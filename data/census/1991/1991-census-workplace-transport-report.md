Preliminary 
1.1 This report was published in July 1991 and contained provisional figures of the population and private households for 
each of the 26 local government districts with a brief statistical commentary. 
Summary 
1.2 The Summary Report was published in October 1992 and contains information for Northern Ireland and for each of the 
26 local government districts. The subjects covered include age distribution, sex, marital status, birthplace, religion, households, 
tenure, amenities, occupation density, availability of cars, communal establishments, economically active population, 
employment status, long-term illness, term-time address of students and schoolchildren, and Irish language. 
Belfast Urban Area 
1.3 The Belfast Urban Area Report was published in December 1992 and contains information to ward level for the Belfast 
Urban Area as defined by the Department of the Environment for Northern Ireland Town and Country Planning Service. The 
subjects covered include age distribution, sex, marital status, birthplace, religion, households, tenure, amenities, occupation 
density, availability of cars, communal establishments, economically active population, employment status, long-term illness, 
term-time address of students and schoolchildren, and Irish language. 
Religion 
1.4 This report was published in February 1993 and contains information at Northern Ireland level and some to local 
government districts on the main religious denominations. The subjects covered include population by age, sex, marital status, 
occupation, industry, and other characteristics. 
Economic Activity 
1.5 This report contains tables giving the figures for both the economically active and economically inactive population. The 
employed population is analysed by industry and occupation classifications and relates to age, marital status, employment status, 
socio-economic groupings, area of workplace, etc. Most tables contain information at Northern Ireland level but some give 
details for local government districts. 
Workplace and Transport to Work 
1.6 Employed population details in relation to area of workplace/residence will be contained in this report. Workplace 
movement by socio-economic group, occupation, industry, and means of transport to work will also be detailed to local 
government district level. 
Housing and Household Composition 
1.7 All tables in this report will contain information at Northern Ireland level but some will give details for local government 
districts. Tables will include information on households, tenure, occupation density, car availability, age, sex, marital status, 
and will relate household composition to social and other economic characteristics. 
Migration 
1.8 Numbers and characteristics of the population who changed their usual address in the year before Census day will be 
contained in this report. Migrant households will be detailed by age, sex, marital status, etc. Some tables will refer to Northern 
Ireland and others will be detailed to local government districts. 

Education 
1.9 This report will contain information at Northern Ireland level by age, sex, occupation, and industry by highest level of 
academic qualification. Also included will be socio-economic group by religion and economic activity by marital status related 
to highest level of academic qualification. 
Irish Language 
1.10 This report will contain information on the population aged 3 years and over, recorded as having knowledge of Irish 
language. The tables covered will include knowledge by age, sex, religion, economic position, and highest level of academic 
qualification to Northern Ireland level.

Introduction 
2.1 This report contains information on the employed population by area of residence and workplace in relation to socio­
economic groups, occupation, industry and means of transport to work. 
Administrative Divisions 
2.2 The local government districts for which statistics are presented in this report are those in existence at the date of the 
Census. 
Population 
2.3 Census results are presented as cross tabulations, each counting a particular population, for example, people, households, 
families, or people in communal establishments. It is important to know exactly what is being counted, i.e. the population base. 
Information on whereabouts and usual address given for each person listed on the Census form can be used to derive the 
population base. 
2.4 The population present on Census night (present population) in an area is the count of all people recorded as 
spending Census night in the area regardless of whether or not this was where they usually lived; that is, all persons recorded 
on the Census H Forms for whom the whereabouts on Census night was given as the relevant household, plus all persons 
recorded as being in communal establishments. 
2.5 The usually resident population in an area is the count of those people recorded on the forms for the area as usually 
living 'at this address' whether or not they are present on Census night. 
2.6 Shipping: Apart from houseboats (enumerated as private households), vessels were treated in a similar way to communal 
establishments with a listing form and individual return form for each person on the ship. Persons on naval vessels in Northern 
Ireland waters or ports on Census night were included as present in the home base port of the ship. Generally persons on other 
vessels were counted as present in the area in which the form was collected. 
2.7 For the 1991 Census there were some minor changes made to the enumeration of shipping. Vessels on coastal trips, 
fishing voyages and voyages between Great Britain and Northern Ireland were not enumerated. However, foreign registered 
ships in Northern Ireland ports, excluded in 1981, were enumerated. 
Campers, persons sleeping rough, etc. 
2.8 The present population also includes persons not enumerated in private households or communal establishments, for 
example, persons sleeping rough and persons enumerated in caravans or other non-permanent structures which were not the 
usual residence. 
Population covered in this Report 
2.9 Figures contained in this report refer to the usually resident population aged 16 years and over employed in a workplace 
in Northern Ireland or elsewhere. In Table 2, information on persons out of employment is also included. 
Households 
2.10 A household is either one person living alone or a group of people (who may or may not be related) living at the same 
address with common housekeeping. Persons staying temporarily with the household are included. A group of people is treated 
as a household if there is any regular arrangement to share at least one meal a day. Two people who share one room, although 
each may have separate catering arrangements, are treated as one household. A household must have exclusive use of at least 
one room.

Age 
2.11 Age is the completed years of age at Census date, as derived from the date of birth question. 
Usual Address 
2.12 In most cases the answer to the usual address question is straightforward. However, students and schoolchildren away 
from home during term-time were to regard their home address as their usual address. Boarders were to decide whether they 
considered as their usual address, the address of the relevant household. Household heads, living away from home for part of 
the week, were to regard their home address as their usual address. 
2.13 Persons who failed to answer the relevant questions appear as part of the usually resident population in the area in which 
they were enumerated and are included as part of a usually resident private household. 
Place of Work 
2.14 Persons in employment were asked to state either (i) the address of workplace or (ii) no fixed place or (iii) mainly at 
home. Those not working regularly at one place or who travel during work were asked to give the address of the depot or 
office to which they report daily. Persons on site work for a long period were asked to give the address of the site. 
Economic Activitj^ 
2.15 The economic activity tables describe some of the economic characteristics of the population. The characteristics 
include both those obtained directly from census questions, for example, economic position, occupation and others derived from 
combining answers to census questions on occupation and industry, for example, employment status. Persons on a Government 
employment or training scheme are regarded as economically active in employment. However, while information about the 
particular scheme was not collected in the Census, persons on these schemes are included as a separate group in tables which 
give details on economic position and occupation. 
2.16 Only specified groups were required to answer some of the economic activity questions and this is reflected in the 
population to which tables refer. Thus: 
(a) Tables relating to economic position include only persons aged 16 years and over. 
(b) Tables relating to occupation cover the economically active. 
(c) Tables relating to employment status cover persons in employment.

Out of employment means seeking work or prevented by temporary sickness from seeking work or waiting to take up

Part-time Workers

Classiflcation of Occupations 
2.20 The occupation of a person is most often determined by reference to that person's main job at the reference time, but 
for persons not currently employed it may be determined by reference to the most recent, or most recent main, job. Any given 
job is assumed to involve a set of typical work activities and to be an instance of a particular occupation. 
2.21 Occupation codes are allocated from the answers to question 17 on the Census form. In the tables, occupations are 
classified according to the Standard Occupational Classification (SOC) 1990 issued by the Office of Population Censuses and 
Surveys and published by HMSO under ISBN 0 11 691284 7(Vol 1), ISBN 0 11 691285 5 (Vol 2) and ISBN 0 11 691338 
X (Vol 3). 
Note: SOC 90 contains new classifications which may differ from those contained in the Classification of Occupations 1980 
(CO 80) which applied to answers given to question 13 on the 1981 Northern Ireland Census form. Consequently it may not 
be possible to make direct comparisons between 1981 and 1991 Tables. 
Principles of the classification 
2.22 The object of the classification is to group together occupations (basically by reference to job titles) which are deemed 
to be similar, taking simultaneous account of 2 main criteria. These criteria are: 
i. the level of skill and/or experience and/or formal qualification which is required to carry out competently the work 
activities typically involved in the occupation; and 
ii. the nature of those work activities. 
2.23 The first criterion - skill level - is applied where possible by reference to the level of formal qualifications currently 
required in order for a person to be recognised as fully competent in the occupation concerned; but in other cases by reference 
to the duration of training and/or experience recognised in the field of employment concerned as being normally required to 
pursue the occupation competently. 
2.24 The second'criterion - nature of work activities - may refer to any or all of a number of aspects of the work, including 
the materials handled, the type of tools and equipment used, and so on. However, it focuses specifically in all cases on work 
activities, rather than on other aspects of the job such as, for example, whether the worker has trainee or supervisory status, 
or what industrial function the job serves. These are ignored by the SOC, - so that, for example, a 'carpenter', an 'apprentice 
or trainee carpenter' and a 'foreman carpenter' are all assigned to exactly the same occupational group. 
Treatment of managerial occupations 
2.25 An area where the nature of the activities, type of skill and status in employment may be difficult to separate 
conceptually is management. The SOC approach, is to treat jobs where the main activity is, 'management above the level of 
a first-line supervisor or foreman', as requiring a special class of skills and thus needing to be classified separately from the 
jobs of the workers managed. Thus in allocating jobs to occupational groups the SOC takes account, in appropriate and defined 
cases, of the designation 'manager' and also of indications that the person is the proprietor of a business. These are treated 
as indications that the job involves activities and skills typical of managers. 

2.26 On the same principle there are a few cases where allocation to an occupational group may be assisted by knowledge 
of whether or not the person has self-employed status. In these limited respects, information on status in employment is taken 
into account in the SOC. 
Treatment of labouring and similar unskilled occupations 
2.27 Jobs having the title 'Labourer' are in general classified, along with other jobs requiring relatively little skill, 
qualification or experience, to Major Group 9 of the SOC. Because of the unspecific terms in which many such jobs are 
reported in practice it is necessary to invoke a quasi-industrial principle for classifying below major group level. This gives 
rise to minor group headings such as: 
*91 Other occupations in mining and manufacturing'. 
'92 Other occupations in construction', and so on. 
2.28 The ultimate recourse is to Minor Group 99 and Unit Group 990 which is a catch-all for 'All other labourers and related 
workers'. The practical implications of this are tending to diminish as the number of generalised unskilled jobs in the economy 
continues to decline. 
Treatment of Armed Forces occupations 
2.29 Members of the Armed Forces may in certain cases have jobs which are unique to that sector of the economy. The 
SOC makes provision for'military occupations specified in terms of ranks (e.g. 'Lieutenant Commander', 'Sergeant' etc.) in 
Minor Groups 15 (Commissioned Officers) and 60 (NCOs and other ranks). In other cases job titles, if given, may equate to 
those of civilian jobs (e.g. vehicle mechanic, radio operator etc.) though of course the persons concerned all hold military ranks 
also. Here the basic classificatory assumption is that both Armed Forces and civilian jobs will be coded to the same groups 
without any distinction being made between them. 
Treatment of agricultural, forestry and Ashing occupations 
2.30 Occupations which are specifically agricultural, forestry and fishing, but not managerial, in nature are allocated to Minor 
Group 90 of SOC and its constituent Unit Groups. This group of occupations is sufficiently distinct from other occupations 
to require its own small set of minor groups and might therefore claim its own major group. However, one criterion for 
distinguishing major groups was that each should contain a substantial proportion of all occupations, and the proportion of the 
labour force currently engaged in agriculture, forestry and fishing is now so small that major group status was judged not to 
be appropriate. 
Classiflcation of Industries 
2.31 The industry in which a person is engaged is determined by the business or industrial activity in which his or her 
occupation is followed. While the occupational classification takes account only of the nature of the work performed the 
industrial classification relates only to the nature of the service or product to which the labour contributes. For example, a 
carpenter woiild be classified industrially to building, if employed by a builder, but to brewing if employed by a brewery firm. 
The allocation of an employed person (a person who had a job at sometime in the week before Census day) to an industry was 
based on the answer given to question 18 on the Census form. Industries are classified in the tables according to the revised 
'Standard Industrial Classification' (SIC) (HMSO 1980 ISBN O 11 6307641) issued by the Central Statistical Office. As SIC 
1980 was also used to classify industries in 1981 Tables, it will be possible to make direct comparisons between those and 1991 
Tables. Paragraphs 2.32-2.47 below are selected extracts from the preface to that publication: 
Introduction 
2.32 The United Kingdom economy is made up of a wide range of economic activities through which goods are produced 
or services rendered by firms and other organisations. For analytical purposes, economic activities of a similar nature may be 
grouped together into 'industries', for example into agriculture, motor vehicle manufacture, retail distribution, catering, national 
government service. A system used to group activities in this way is described as an industrial classification. 'Industry' in this 
context is not restricted to extractive or production activities but extends to the provision of goods and services of all kinds. 
2.33 Such a classification usually starts with a small number of broad groups of activities which are then subdivided into 
progressively narrower groups so that the classification can be used with varying amounts of detail for different purposes. Thus 
a broad group 'Transport and communication' may be subdivided first into the different modes of transport (road, rail, water. 

air) and communication (postal services, telecommunications) and these in turn into smaller subdivisions such as road haulage, 
bus and coach services etc. 
The basis and structure of the classiflcation 
2.34 The classification is intended to apply to units about which important economic statistics are collected. A number of 
different factors have been taken into account in the classification. They include the nature of the process or of the work done, 
the principal raw material used, the type or intended use of goods produced or handled, and the type of service rendered. 
Different factors predominate in different parts of the classification and in many cases a broad group based on one common 
factor is subdivided by reference to another. The different headings of the classification (i.e. the short titles of the industries) 
are accordingly worded so as to indicate the factor or combination of factors on which they are based and this is followed in 
each case by a brief description of the activities included. 
2.35 The full range of activities is first divided into 10 broad Divisions (listed in paragraph 12 below) each denoted by a 
single digit from 0 to 9. The Divisions are in turn subdivided into Classes (each denoted by the addition of a second digit), 
the Classes into Groups (3 digits) and the Groups into Activity headings (4 digits). There are 10 Divisions, 60 Classes, 222 
Groups and 334 Activity headings. The full number of possible subdivisions at each level is not necessarily used but is varied 
according to the diversity of activities included. For example. Division 0 Agriculture, Forestry and Fishing is divided into only 
3 Classes, without further subdivisions, whereas Division 4 Other Manufacturing Industries has 8 Classes, 50 Groups and 91 
Activity headings. The use of 0 as the final digit for a Class, Group or Activity heading normally indicates that it is the only 
subdivision at that level, thus Group 140 is the only Group in Class 14 (Mineral oil processing) but is itself divided into 2 
Activity headings, 1401 and 1402. Class 16, (Production and distribution of electricity, gas and other forms of energy) on the 
other hand has 3 Groups which are therefore numbered 161, 162 and 163 but since one of these is further subdivided the 
Activity headings are numbered 1610, 1620 and 1630... 
Statistical units 
2.36 The classification is applied to units on the basis of their principal activity. Frequently no problem arises in defining 
the relevant unit. This will be the case, for example, where the unit is situated at a single address, is mainly engaged in a single 
activity (or group of activities falling within the same heading of the classification) and is operated as a separate entity for the 
purposes of record keeping and accounting (including VAT returns). 
2.37 In many cases, however, the situation is more complex. Many units produce, sell or provide a variety of goods or 
services, some of which are considered to be appropriate to one heading of the classification and some to another. In such 
cases the unit as a whole is classified according to the type of goods or services which make up the greater part of its 
production, sales or service. In government inquiries a unit's classification is made on the basis of information relating to the 
unit which is supplied on the particular return or which is already available from other inquiries. In some cases classification 
may be based on the unit's own description of its principal activity. 
2.38 The units to be classified may vary according to the type of data being collected and the purposes for which the data 
are required. In view of this it is important that when analyses by industry are prepared the statistical unit used for the inquiry 
concerned should be defined. Sometimes, in order to secure comparability between the results of different inquiries, it may be 
necessary to give some units more than one classification. This may occur, for example, when a unit at one address can provide 
a limited range of data, say, employment but for the provision of more comprehensive data has to be combined with another 
unit, or units, under the same ownership which are mainly engaged in a different activity. 
2.39 Many businesses produce for their own use goods or services which are normally produced for sale by other industries. 
Typical examples are the manufacture or printing of containers for their own products, the generation of electricity, research, 
the repair and maintenance of buildings and equipment, and, occasionally, the construction or extension of buildings. If the 
relevant range of information is available these ancillary activities are treated as separate units and classified according to their 
own activity. But frequently the necessary data is not available, and the ancillary activities cannot be distinguished for most 
statistical purposes. When ancillary activities are carried out at separate locations it may be possible to treat them as units for 
a limited range of data e.g. for the census of employment. 
2.40 The sales staff of a manufacturing business are not treated as a separate establishment unless separate wholesale or retail 
outlets are operated and complete accounts are kept for them. 
2.41 The classification of head offices or other central offices at separate locations from those at which the main activities 
of the business are carried out is determined by the nature of the activities of the units which they serve. If those units are 
all classified to the same industry there is no problem, but if they are classified to 2 or more different industries, the

classification of the head office, etc., is that of the largest part of the activities. Exceptionally, if the activities are very diffuse, 
the central office is classified in heading 8396 - Central offices not allocatable elsewhere - which is also used for the UK 
offices of businesses operating mainly abroad. 
Some special points 
2.42 Steel - An exception to the rule of classification according to major activity is that reporting units whose activities 
include the basic manufacture of iron and steel as defined in the ECSC Treaty, are classified to heading 2210 - Iron and steel 
industry. 
2.43 Merchanting activities - Establishments mainly engaged in merchanting, broking, importing and exporting are classified 
to the appropriate heading in the distributive trades, not to the industry or trade producing the goods in which they deal. 
2.44 Repair Work - Much repair work is carried out either by the manufacturers or by the distributors of the goods 
concerned, or of similar goods, and in such cases it is rarely possible to distinguish a separate unit engaged mainly in repair 
work. There are, however, numerous units which specialise in repair work, mainly of consumer goods - motor vehicles, 
footwear, watches and clocks, television and radio sets, etc. These specialist repair activities are classified separately. The 
repair of capital equipment, such as ships, aircraft, and most types of plant and machinery is classified with the corresponding 
manufacture, even if carried out by specialist units. Units engaged in the repair and maintenance of buildings are classified 
to Construction. 
2.45 Construction - In principle all construction work should be classified to Class 50 - Construction. In practice it is 
impossible to obtain a ^ull range of economic statistics for construction work done by all directly employed labour. These 
activities will generally be classified to the same heading as the establishment which they serve. The construction activities 
of direct labour forces employed by local authorities and government departments, using dh-ect labour are, however, allocated 
to Class 50. 
2.46 Installation Work - The installation of machinery, equipment, etc. is normally carried out by the manufacturer or 
distributor and is not distinguishable as a separate activity. There are some business units, however, where installation is the 
only, or the major activity. Such units are classified according to the type of equipment involved as follows: 
i. The installation of items which can be regarded as forming an integral part of a building, e.g. an electrical wiring *. 
system, heating and ventilating apparatus, is classified to Construction. 
ii. The installation of equipment which requires little more than the connection of the equipment to the mains, e.g. of 
domestic washing machines, television sets, is classified to Distribution. 
iii. The installation of plant and machinery, e.g. a computer is... classified with the manufacturer of similar articles...
